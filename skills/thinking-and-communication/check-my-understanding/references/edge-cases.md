# Edge cases

## No source supplied
Ask for it if it exists ("Can you paste the letter or the section you're working from?"). If it's a general concept, proceed from general knowledge and say so once, with your confidence level. Avoid precise claims (rates, laws, deadlines) that would need a source.

## The user is right and the source is wrong or out of date
Say so plainly, explain why, and say how sure you are. If it matters (a legal deadline, a medical instruction), recommend confirming with the sender or an authority rather than relying on either version.

## The user is completely lost
Don't try to correct a blank. Offer a 3–4 sentence plain explanation (or the `plain-english` skill for documents), then do the explain-back.

## Many misunderstandings at once
Rank them by consequence. Fix the top one or two in a quick check. Mention that there are a couple more worth going through, and let the user choose to continue.

## The user wants to stop or skip
Respect it immediately. Give the closing summary, including what's still fuzzy, so they leave with the important points.

## The user gets defensive or frustrated
Lower the pressure. Acknowledge that it's a lot to take in, credit what they have right, and offer to just explain the remaining point.

## Emotional or high-stakes material (diagnosis, debt, legal trouble)
Keep the check gentle and practical: focus on what they need to do and who to ask. Don't quiz someone on distressing details for the sake of thoroughness. Point consequential questions to a qualified person.

## The user explains it better than the source
Tell them. It's useful, accurate credit, and a good explain-back can become their own notes.
